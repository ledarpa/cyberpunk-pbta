# System

## How to Play

This game is a PbtA variant (*Powered by the Apocalypse*).

The golden rule: **fiction first, rules second.**
Nobody "triggers a Move" by announcing its name. The character **does something** in the scene. If that fits a Move, it resolves with those rules.

The table is a conversation:

1. The Game Master (GM) describes the situation: what you see, what threatens, what squeezes.
2. A player says what their character does (with enough detail to picture it).
3. The GM answers in one of these ways:
   - **No roll** (it's safe, obvious, or there's no interesting cost), or
   - **There's a Move / a roll** (there's risk, uncertainty, or consequences that matter).
4. The result applies and the fiction changes.
5. Back to step 1.

The story doesn't stall on a miss. A 6 or less **opens up** danger, cost, or fresh pressure.

> **Example — The miss that opens the game**
> Dante, Mercenary, is crawling through the vent duct on the 40th floor of the Biotechnica tower. Below, the lab. Behind him, nothing.
>
> —I slide over to the grate and pop it open without a sound.
>
> —This Move needs precision: roll 2d6 + your Kinetic Response modifier (2d6 + KR).
>
> The dice roll. A 2 and a 3. With the +1: **6**. Table goes quiet. Dante picks the consequence: *someone already knew he was coming*.
>
> "I press my fingers against the grate and I feel the mistake before I see it: the screws are already loose. Someone pulled them out and put them back in. The grate drops with a dry clang, and when I look down there are three guards below, staring up, smiling. One of them talks into his radio: 'Yeah, that's him. The guy from the photo.'"
>
> The scene didn't die: a worse, better one opened up. Now Dante knows something terrible and priceless: **he was sold out.** Someone inside his own network talked. A 6− isn't a wall, it's a door into a darker room. The extraction clock starts running, the betrayal is still waiting on a name, and the session just gained a new engine.
>
> Failing isn't hitting the brakes. It's flooring the gas with the car on fire.

**Who rolls:** the players. The GM doesn't roll dice to "win" — they speak for the world and apply consequences.

## When to Roll (and When Not To)

**You roll** when the character's action carries **risk**, **uncertainty**, or **serious consequences**.

**You don't roll** when the outcome is clear and boring: opening an unwatched door, walking an empty corridor, buying a coffee. If there's no pressure, the Player narrates, the GM adds color, done.

A useful question at the table: *"What could go wrong in an interesting way if this fails or half-lands?"*
If the answer is "nothing," don't roll.

> **Example — When not to roll**
> Three in the morning. The Synthetic Ramen joint on 22nd Street, greasy steam against the neon. Vera walks in, orders the usual, takes the back booth.
>
> The guy next to her has his arm chromed up to the shoulder and the face of someone who hasn't slept since Tuesday. He looks up. Stares at her. One long second, the kind that's worth money on the street.
>
> —Careful —says the player—. I shift my bag, just in case.
>
> —Don't roll anything —says the GM—. The guy looks at you, yawns, and goes back to his soup. He's missing a tooth and he's got years to spare.
>
> And it goes on: the guy pays, walks out into the rain, and Vera finishes her ramen watching the cables hang from the awning like dead vines. Nothing happened. Precisely because: no dice.
>
> If nothing interesting can go wrong, there's no roll. The Player narrates, the scene breathes. Always ask yourself: *what breaks if this fails?* If the answer is "nothing," move along. Dice are for when the street bites, not for when it yawns.

## The Four Attributes

Every character has four attributes. When a roll is called for, you add whichever fits **how** the character acts in the fiction.

| Code | Name | Use it when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **NE** | Neural Engine | You think, remember, hack, run systems, use implants/tech |
| **CM** | Cognitive Manipulation | You read people, persuade, lie, apply social pressure, empathize |
| **KR** | Kinetic Response | Reflexes, precision, dodging, acting under fire, timing |
| **MT** | Muscular Tissue | Strength, endurance, physical violence, surviving extreme conditions |

If you're torn between two, pick the one that describes **the method** (do you force the door with your body, or open it with a digital tool?).

## Rolls (2d6 + Attribute)

When a roll is called for: **2d6 + the attribute**.

| Total | Name | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **10+** | Full success | You get what you were after. No heavy cost (a minor detail can show up, not a twist that sinks you). |
| **7–9** | Partial success | **You get the goal**, but you pay a price: complication, ugly choice, damage, time, noise, debt, enemy attention. |
| **6 or less** | Failure / mess | The GM makes the scene concretely worse. You may not get what you wanted, or you get it too late / too expensive. The game **goes on**. |

The 7–9 is the most common result and the one that moves the story hardest: you get something **and** the world pushes back.

Quick example:

> You want to clear the perimeter wall before the drone lights you up.
> You roll **2d6 + KR**.
> - **10+:** you clear it clean; the drone never tags you.
> - **7–9:** you clear it, but the drone catches you for a second: local alarm, or you scrape yourself, or you drop a piece of kit on the climb.
> - **6−:** you don't make it in time / you're seen / you land bad. The GM says how the scene gets worse.

> **Example — The table loop**
> Arasaka depot, Watson docks. Rain. Nix (Netrunner) needs to slip through the service door without the camera tagging him. Around the table, three empty beer cans and mugs of reheated coffee.
>
> —I go for the door. Slow, careful.
>
> —There's a rotating camera and a guard glued to the HUD scan. This Move needs precision: roll 2d6 + your Kinetic Response modifier (2d6 + KR).
>
> **If you roll 10+** (an 11 comes up), you narrate, clean:
>
> "I wait out the camera sweep counting in my head. Three, two… I press flat against the wall right in the blind spot, cross in two strides, and the door gives with a click. Inside. My jacket didn't even wrinkle."
>
> **If you roll 7–9** (an 8), the GM gives you a choice: *the camera tags you for a second, you leave prints on the doorknob, the guard turns and spots you from afar, or the audio alarm trips.*
>
> —I'll take it: the camera tagged me.
>
> "Perfect crossing, inside, the door closes… and in the glass reflection I see the camera's red eye locked on me for one full second. I'm in, but Arasaka already knows someone's here."
>
> **If you roll 6−** (a 5), you pick a consequence and narrate the flop on top of it:
>
> —The alarm.
>
> "I step wrong, the sheet metal crunches, the camera swings and drowns me in white light. I run, dive behind a container, and hear the siren waking up overhead. The plan is dead. Now we improvise."

## Moves

A **Move** is a rule shaped like this:

- **When** (a trigger in the fiction),
- **you roll** (if it applies),
- **what happens** on 10+, 7–9 and (sometimes) 6−.

If the character performs the trigger's action, **the Move happens**. You can't "do that" in the fiction and skip the rule.

- It's used when there's risk, uncertainty, or consequences that matter.
- Any character can attempt whatever the fiction allows.
- What sets characters apart isn't "who's allowed to try," it's **what tools** they solve it with (attributes, chrome, hardware, position, allies).

When a Move lists options (pick 1, pick 2, ask X), follow the Move's text. If it doesn't list the 6−, the GM applies a hard, direct consequence that fits the scene.

## The GM's Job

The GM doesn't compete against the table. The job:

- Bring Night City to the table: threats, people, systems, clocks that keep moving.
- Ask and clarify the fiction (*"how do you do it?"*, *"which piece of chrome or which weapon?"*).
- Call whether a roll happens or not.
- Apply results, especially on **7–9** and **6−**: more danger, bad intel, time running out, security waking up, enemy reaction, humanity cost, and so on.

On a 7–9, offer a clear price.
On a 6−, change the state of the game: something is lost, someone finds out, the clock moves, worse opposition shows up.

## Chrome and Hardware

Two kinds of tools define power in Night City:

### Chrome

| Aspect | Chrome |
| --- | --- |
| What it is | Implants and tech **inside the body** |
| In the fiction | Justifies integrated "superhuman" capabilities |
| Rules | Humanity, @Psyche, catalog in *Cyberware* |

### Hardware

| Aspect | Hardware |
| --- | --- |
| What it is | **External** kit: weapons, armor, tools, vehicles |
| In the fiction | Justifies what you carry, hide, or deploy |
| Rules | No @Psyche; catalog in *Hardware* |

Many advanced actions demand the right implant or piece of kit, on top of the roll.

Characters don't advance by raising attributes. To grow, you need better chrome, better hardware, and resources (see *Neurodata, Stars and Credits*). An infiltrator with camouflage and an armored merc resolve the same Moves in very different ways.

---

## Campaign Tone

High-stakes stories: corporations, modified bodies, violent streets. Decisions matter. Every roll builds survival, ambition, and how much human you've got left.

At the table, creativity and smart use of chrome and hardware weigh as much as the numbers on the sheet.
